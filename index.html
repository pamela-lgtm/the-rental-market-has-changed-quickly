We are going to be direct with you

Something has changed in this market over the last four to six weeks, and it changed faster than any of us expected. We are not going to soften it, and we are not going to wait another month hoping it corrects on its own.

The single number that concerns us most is not the application count. It is the September move-ins. By the last week of August in a normal year we have ten to twelve residents scheduled to move in the following month. This year we have one.

Our read

What we believe is happening

We will be clear that this next part is our interpretation rather than established fact. In this business you usually learn the real reason in hindsight. But the direction is not in question, and four things appear to be happening at once.

The seasonal window has closed, and it closed hard

Rental demand in Central Florida runs heavily on the school calendar. Families relocating for the school year finish moving in July and early August. From here into the holidays, fewer households move — and the ones who do have more homes to choose from.

The sales market is pushing inventory into the rental market

This is the piece we think matters most, and it is accelerating. Homes that do not sell are becoming rentals. Every one of them becomes a competitor for your resident, and they arrive on the market every single week. We are seeing this directly in our own comparable research — properties listed for sale for months, withdrawn, and re-listed for rent within days.

Resale homes are stalling because they are competing with builders

Builders across Central Florida are discounting aggressively to keep absorption up — rate buydowns, five-figure closing credits, waived upgrades. A resale seller cannot match a two-point rate buydown. So the resale home sits, the seller runs out of patience, and the home becomes a rental.

In some Orlando growth corridors more than half of active listings are now carrying price cuts, and correctly priced homes are selling in a fraction of the time overpriced ones take. The same principle is now governing the rental side.

Supply is rising while demand thins — at the same time

Either one of those alone would soften pricing. Together they compress it quickly. We do not expect rental inventory in our corridor to be smaller in November than it is today. We expect it to be larger.

Where this leaves your analysis

If we gave you numbers in June or July, those numbers are now out of date

Some of you received a rental analysis from us earlier this summer. Those analyses were accurate when we wrote them — built on real leases that had actually closed in the preceding weeks. That is the correct way to price a property.

But a rental analysis is a photograph, not a forecast. The market it described no longer exists. A June or July analysis was reading a market with roughly four times the application volume we are seeing this month, and a fall that had not yet begun filling with converted sale listings.

Including any low end of the range we discussed

If we walked through a price range with you and identified a lower figure we expected to need, that figure was drawn from the same closed-lease data as the rest of the analysis. It was the right read of the market as it existed in June and July.

What has happened since is an anomaly. After more than twenty years in this business, this is one of the most marked slowdowns we have seen — and no data set tells you in advance when a market is about to behave unusually. That is precisely why we now hold the low end of any range as a step rather than a stopping point. We do not get to decide where the bottom is. Only the market does, and it will tell us in real time. What we commit to is reading it honestly every week and moving with it, as far as it asks us to and for as long as it asks.

We would rather revise a recommendation in front of you than defend a number the market stopped supporting six weeks ago. Pricing is not a position we take and hold. It is a reading we update.

Why this matters more than the rent number

We are not trying to find you an applicant. We are trying to find you a choice.

This is the part we most want you to hear, because it is where our interests and yours are completely aligned.

A property priced at the edge of the market will eventually produce an application. One. And here is what happens next: we screen it against the same written standard we apply to every applicant, and if it does not meet that standard, we decline it. We do not lower the bar because the pool is thin. Every property we manage is capable of attracting a well-qualified resident, and we do not shortcut around that for anyone.

So the cost of a thin applicant pool is not a bad resident. It is time. We decline, and then we wait for the next single applicant — who in this market may be three or four weeks away, and who may not qualify either. That is how a property quietly loses two and three months while everyone involved is doing exactly the right thing.

What correct pricing actually buys you

Enough qualified interest that we can choose rather than simply wait. Our screening standard does not move with the market — verified income, stable employment, solid credit, clean rental history, applied identically to every applicant on every property. What changes with the market is how many people clear it in a given month.

When several qualified applicants come forward, we select the strongest and your home is leased. When one comes forward and does not qualify, we decline and the clock keeps running. Correct pricing is what puts enough people in front of that standard for it to produce a resident instead of a delay.

Holding a high price does not protect your income. It narrows the pool until qualified applicants stop appearing at all — and then it charges you for every week you spend waiting on the next one.

The hidden cost

Every day on the market works against you, whether or not price is the reason

Zillow, Apartments.com and the rest publish your days on market in plain sight. Prospective residents see that number before they see a single photograph.

What they conclude is not "that owner is holding firm on price." What they conclude is "something must be wrong with that one." Then they scroll past. It does not matter that the home is immaculate and the price is only slightly high — the counter has already made the argument for them.

That is the compounding problem. A listing that sits gets fewer views because it has been sitting, which makes it sit longer. By the time the price is corrected, the audience that would have rented it has already moved on and signed somewhere else. We would rather reduce early, while the listing still has attention, than reduce late into a market that has already dismissed it.

The trap we are trying to keep you out of

List high, wait, reduce, wait, reduce again — and end up leasing in November at a number below what a decisive reduction would have achieved in September, after two months of vacancy. We have watched this happen to comparable properties in our own corridor this summer, more than once. The homes that leased quickly did not lease cheaply. They leased because they were priced correctly the moment the market looked at them.

What vacancy actually costs

The arithmetic almost always favors moving

Take a home at $3,000 a month. Every vacant month costs $3,000 that is gone permanently — it does not come back later in the lease.

  • A $100 reduction costs $1,200 over a twelve-month lease.
  • A $150 reduction costs $1,800 over the same lease.
  • One additional vacant month costs $3,000 — more than either, and you still have to lease the home afterward, into a market with more competition than today.

Put plainly: a meaningful reduction pays for itself if it saves you even one month of vacancy. In this market, it will usually save more than one.

Our position

We cannot control the market. We can only respond to it.

No company can command a higher rent than the market will pay. Any firm that tells you otherwise is selling you something. The rent is set by what a resident is willing to pay this month, for a home like yours, in a neighborhood like yours — and that number has moved.

What we can do is read it accurately, tell you the truth about it quickly, and act while acting still helps. Waiting is not a neutral choice. It is a choice to pay for the delay in vacant months and in a weaker applicant pool when the home finally does lease.

What we will not do

We will not tell you the market is fine when it is not. We will not sit on a listing and hope. We will not promise you a rent number or a time to lease, because nobody honestly can. And we will never recommend lowering the screening standard because an applicant is the only one who came forward. That standard is not negotiable on any property we manage — which is exactly why the price has to be right.
